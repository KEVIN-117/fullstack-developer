## **The Inbox Problem**

The startup team had successfully implemented an AI support agent, refined its training, and expanded its vector database. The RAG solution was working wonders on the website, and for the first time, the team was ahead of the support queue.

Emboldened by this success, the agent was extended. Why not directly sort the incoming support email by topic and severity? This would free up even more precious time. The initial rollout was promising. Standard support queries were handled with increasing efficiency, deflecting many before they even hit a human inbox.

But extending the agent to the email front opened a new, unexpected floodgate. It wasn’t just support requests landing in the inbox anymore. There were sales inquiries (“Tell me about your enterprise plan . . .”), partnership proposals (“We think our companies could collaborate on . . .”), press requests (“Would you be available for an interview about your viral launch?”), and of course, the never-ending tide of spam (“Boost your SEO with . . .”).

The team was suddenly drowning again, but in a completely new way. The AI agent, trained to answer support questions, was staring at a chaotic mix. A typical Monday now saw a backlog of 1,200 messages that the agent couldn’t handle, forcing engineers to log weekend hours simply to triage the inbox.

Although the agent could handle the content of some emails, the bigger problem was not answering them. It was understanding what they were. Was this a hot lead or just another spammer? Was this a critical support issue or a vendor pitch?

What was desperately needed was an intelligent mail-room clerk, one who could look at every incoming email and instantly slap a label on it, a label that was not generic but specific to this company’s needs: Sales Lead, Customer Support, Partnership Idea, or simply Junk: Ignore. Anything that did not fit a key category needed to be set aside and marked as Unread for human review.

The existing system, as powerful as it was in retrieving information and generating responses, did not have this innate understanding of the types of conversations the business had. It knew about the product, but it did not know how to classify the intention behind an email based on the subtle nuances of language used specifically in sales pitches versus support tickets versus genuine press inquiries directed at their business.

Could a model be taught these distinctions? Could it learn to recognize the patterns, the keywords, the structures that screamed “Sales!” or whispered “Support”? Could it be molded, specifically and precisely, to sort this unique deluge of company communications?

This challenge led the team down a new path, one that promised a deeper level of control and customization over the AI’s intelligence. This was the path of _fine-tuning_.

Following our ongoing example of building an AI support agent, a practical application for fine-tuning is training a model to automatically classify incoming email. Imagine teaching the AI to sort email into categories like Inquiry, Support Request, Billing Question, or General Feedback.

To fine-tune a model to accurately perform this specific task, we first need a dataset consisting of many example emails, each correctly tagged with the category it belongs to. The challenge is obtaining a dataset that genuinely represents the variety and nuances of the email your AI agent will encounter. While it’s possible to use a powerful LLM to generate synthetic, or made-up, emails, relying solely on this approach has potential drawbacks.

If your goal is to fine-tune a model that performs as well as, or even surpasses, large commercial models like those from Google or OpenAI on real-world, unseen email, training only on data generated by those same models might not give you an actual test of its ability on novel examples. Your fine-tuned model might become very good at classifying email similar to the synthetic ones, but its performance on authentic email it hasn’t seen before could be uncertain.

For a practical and reliable dataset, the best resource is often real data that reflects the actual task—in this case, your own email.

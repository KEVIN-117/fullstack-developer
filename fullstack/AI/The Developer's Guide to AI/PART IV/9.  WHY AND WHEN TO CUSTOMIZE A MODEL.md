When you use a model via Ollama or Hugging Face, or when you interact with ChatGPT or Gemini, you are interacting with a pretrained model. A pretrained model has “acquired” the skills from a set of data that was provided to it by someone or by a team. These kinds of pretrained generic models save time and effort because we don’t have to build foundational models from scratch.

For most generic tasks, building a new model isn’t necessary; modern AI has reached a level of proficiency that handles standard requirements with ease. This creates a vast landscape for custom applications using pretrained models. To highlight two such broad scenarios:

- If your task involves asking questions about widely known information, generating creative text, summarizing general topics, translating languages, or rephrasing content in a different style that’s within the LLM’s standard capabilities, you can often use just prompt engineering to achieve excellent results.
- When you need the LLM to answer questions or generate content based on information that was not in its original training data—such as recent news events, documents specific to your organization, or details from a particular report, RAG is the ideal approach.

But now, imagine you are working for a company in a very specialized industry, perhaps manufacturing, where internal reports follow a highly specific template, use proprietary jargon extensively, and require data to be presented in a unique, structured format.

You want AI to automate the generation of draft reports based on raw input data. While a base LLM can understand and generate language, and RAG could help the LLM access specific data points, a pretrained model would struggle to consistently apply the precise, internal formatting rules, use the proprietary terminology correctly in context, and maintain the required structure that deviates significantly from its general training data. Prompting can help to a degree, but for true consistency and accuracy within this narrow, technical niche, the base model’s general-purpose training wouldn’t be sufficient.

When faced with a situation such as this, where a standard pretrained model does not possess the necessary deep specialization or the ability to reliably follow highly specific, nonstandard requirements through prompting or RAG, you have two ways forward:

- Build a new model from scratch.
- Fine-tune a pretrained model.

In this chapter, we will look at the basic differences between building a new model and fine-tuning an existing one. The approach is not one-size-fits-all; choosing one over the other depends on multiple factors. Let’s dive in.

### **Building a New Model vs. Fine-Tuning**

The choice of building a new model or fine-tuning an existing model is a crucial decision while building your application. Each approach has advantages and disadvantages, and the best choice often depends on the specific task, available resources, and desired performance.

Building a new model ([Figure 9-1](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch9.xhtml#fig9_1)) offers complete control and customization but requires extensive data, computational resources, and expertise.

Fine-tuning a pretrained model ([Figure 9-2](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch9.xhtml#fig9_2)), on the other hand, leverages existing knowledge for faster training and improved performance with less data, but offers less control and may carry biases from the original training data.

In the context of AI, _bias_ refers to systematic errors that occur when a model produces consistently distorted results or unfair prejudices. It typically stems from training data that reflects human stereotypes, historical inequalities, or unrepresentative samples. Consequently, these biases lead to skewed outputs.

Build a new model when you have the following:

- A very specific task with unique requirements

### **Fine-Tuning Strategies**

Adapting a powerful pretrained model to excel at specific tasks is a key part of building customized AI solutions. However, achieving this specialization isn’t a one-size-fits-all process.

Three primary strategies are commonly used for fine-tuning LLMs, each varying in how data is labeled and how the model learns from feedback:

- Self-supervised fine-tuning
- Supervised fine-tuning
- Reinforcement learning fine-tuning

#### _**Self-Supervised Fine-Tuning**_

Self-supervised fine-tuning involves learning from patterns. Imagine teaching a child by giving them a vast library and asking them to guess the next word in every book they read. This is the essence of self-supervised learning, the very method used to train the foundational language models we use today. The model learns by identifying patterns and relationships within massive amounts of text, essentially predicting what comes next.

When it comes to fine-tuning, this approach allows for a high degree of customization. By carefully selecting the text the model learns from, you can tailor it to specific tasks or styles. For instance, if you want your language model to mimic your unique writing voice, you could fine-tune it on a collection of your own written works. This allows the model to absorb and replicate the subtle nuances of your style, making its output remarkably personalized.

#### _**Supervised Fine-Tuning**_

Supervised fine-tuning, which involves learning from examples, takes a more direct approach, akin to teaching with flashcards. In this method, the model learns from a dataset that pairs each piece of information with a corresponding label or answer.

Think of this strategy as providing the model with clear examples of input and desired output. For example, if you want to improve a model’s ability to answer questions, you could fine-tune it on a dataset of question-answer pairs. The model learns to associate specific questions with their correct answers, enhancing its accuracy and responsiveness. This is a powerful technique for tasks where clear input-output relationships exist, allowing for precise control over the model’s behavior.

#### _**Reinforcement Learning Fine-Tuning**_

Reinforcement learning fine-tuning introduces a feedback loop, similar to a teacher guiding a student through iterative revisions. In this approach, a separate “reward model” is trained to evaluate the quality of the language model’s output. This reward model provides feedback, essentially ranking the model’s responses and guiding it toward generating better results.

OpenAI used this approach to develop InstructGPT. Human labelers rated the model’s outputs, and these ratings were used to train the reward model. By incorporating human preferences and judgments, reinforcement learning allows for fine-tuning that aligns with complex, subjective criteria, leading to more nuanced and desirable outputs.

### **Fine-Tuning Methods**

Having explored the various learning strategies used in fine-tuning LLMs, we now shift our focus to the practical side: How do you actually perform the fine-tuning process? This section covers fine-tuning methods, exploring the options available for implementing fine-tuning based on your technical comfort and desired control. These methods primarily differ in the level of technical detail and coding required, ranging from simplified platforms to hands-on coding pipelines.

Depending on your technical background and the level of control you desire, you can fine-tune a model in primarily two ways. In a _no-code approach_, you use a platform that significantly simplifies the technical steps. In a more _technical (code-based) approach_, you write code and manage details like data preparation, configuration, and the training process yourself.

If this is your first time fine-tuning or you prefer a quicker way to get hands-on experience, the no-code option is recommended. However, while it is simpler, this ease comes from hiding much of the underlying complexity, giving you less insight into the technical details of how the model is trained. To cover both methods, we will first walk through the no-code fine-tuning process, followed by a deeper dive into the more technical, code-based approach.

#### _**No-Code Fine-Tuning**_

Stepping into fine-tuning does not necessarily require deep expertise in ML code and infrastructure right away. The no-code approach simplifies the process dramatically by providing specialized tools, often with intuitive graphical interfaces. These tools abstract away the underlying complexities of setting up training environments, managing hardware, and writing training loops, allowing you to focus more directly on your data and the desired outcome for your model.

#### **Hugging Face AutoTrain**

A widely used and excellent no-code fine-tuning example is Hugging Face AutoTrain (_[https://huggingface.co/docs/autotrain/index](https://huggingface.co/docs/autotrain/index)_). Available as a hosted workspace that you can access online or as software you can download and run on your own machine, AutoTrain guides you through the essential steps with a straightforward interface.

You simply upload your prepared training and validation dataset files, select the base model you want to fine-tune from a list, and configure key training parameters by using point-and-click options or simple forms. Auto-Train then handles the heavy lifting of running the training process in the background. For users who prefer scripting, AutoTrain also offers a CLI-based approach (see [Figure 9-3](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch9.xhtml#fig9_3)).

![Screenshot showing fields for project name, base model, dataset source, training data upload, column mapping, and parameter settings.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page234_1.jpg)

_Figure 9-3: Hugging Face AutoTrain_

Using this interface, you just have to select the model, provide the training and validation data, and set the parameters to tune.

#### **Command Line Options**

You could also provide the same set of options via the command line:

autotrain text-classification \
--train \
--model distilbert/distilbert-base-uncased \
--project-name my-mail-classifier \
--data-path data/ \
--train-split train \
--valid-split valid \
--text-column text \
--target-column label \
--epochs 3 \
--batch-size 4 \
--lr 2e-5 \
--optimizer adamw_torch \
--scheduler linear \
--gradient-accumulation 1 \
--mixed-precision fp16

_Listing 9-1: AutoTrain CLI mode_

While the CLI is a great shortcut for launching experiments, it often acts as a “black box” that hides the internal logic and limits your ability to tweak the details. To truly unlock what your models can do, it’s time to move beyond predefined commands and into a more hands-on approach that gives you total control over every step of the process.

#### _**Technical Fine-Tuning**_

Stepping away from the simplified interfaces of no-code tools, the technical fine-tuning approach offers significantly more control over the entire process. This hands-on approach allows you to precisely manage data preparation, configure detailed training parameters, and gain a much deeper understanding of the underlying mechanics of model training.

However, this increased control and flexibility requires a greater time investment to learn the relevant concepts and to write code for building and managing the fine-tuning pipeline. We’ll utilize the Hugging Face libraries to demonstrate this process (we will dive deep into this in [Chapter 11](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch11.xhtml#ch11)). As you learn the practical aspects of fine-tuning via code, we’ll guide you through the following:

- **Preparing your datasets** Learning how to load, process, and format your data correctly for model training with code, and understanding important considerations involved in creating a robust dataset
- **Fine-tuning a standard classification model** Using a model like Distil-BERT as a concrete, simpler example to illustrate the fundamental steps of training a model for a specific text-classification task
- **Fine-tuning an LLM** Exploring how fine-tuning an LLM differs significantly from fine-tuning a smaller classification model, and understanding the specific types of data and techniques used for advanced LLM customization

While the core libraries are powerful, you’ll often want to use open source utilities specifically built for fast and memory-efficient fine-tuning. This is a rapidly evolving area in AI, with new tools appearing all the time. These utilities are cleverly designed to make customizing models much less demanding on your computer’s memory. A popular example is Unsloth (_[https://unsloth.ai](https://unsloth.ai/)_), which is designed to make fine-tuning incredibly fast. Tools like this are worth exploring as they can significantly lower the hardware barrier for your projects.

### **The Fine-Tuning Process**

Independent of which approach you take for fine-tuning, the training process has three main steps. These steps are applicable not just for fine-tuning, but also for building your model:

1. Prepare the data.
2. Fine-tune the model.
3. Test the new model.

That’s it. Three easy steps.

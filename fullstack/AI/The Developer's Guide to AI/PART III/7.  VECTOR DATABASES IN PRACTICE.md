Now it’s time to expand our view of pre-trained AI models beyond LLMs. Embedding models are the quiet workhorses behind many of the “How did it know that?” experiences in modern software. These models help you find the right information instantly by searching for meaning instead of exact keywords, so the right product, support ticket, or insight surfaces even when the wording is different.

### **Helping Machines Understand Our World**

You’ve seen how LLMs seem to understand natural language, but how do they work? Machines are literal. They “think” in 1s and 0s. Natural language is complex and nuanced. Words take on a different meaning depending on the context. Take the following sentences:

- “I’m going to the _bank_ to deposit some money.”
- “You’ll catch more fish in this river if you walk down to the _bank_.”

The word _bank_ has a completely different meaning in each case. In the first sentence, we’re obviously talking about a financial institution, but in the second sentence, we’re referring to a riverbank. We understand what bank we’re referring to based on the entire sentence, the context.

If we thought like a machine, we would make some mistakes ([Figure 7-1](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#fig7_1)).

![Two scenes show a person mentioning “bank” for depositing money and fishing, with a robot interpreting both as a financial building.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page162_1.jpg)

_Figure 7-1: A machine confusing the word_ bank _would mean a disappointing fishing trip._

For a machine to understand natural language, we need a way to encode words along with their contextual meaning into a machine-friendly representation. That’s exactly what text embedding models are trained to do.

Just like LLMs, these embedding models are trained on large amounts of freely available and licensed data. They capture meaning based on the way words are used together in different contexts. These models are a foundational component of LLMs, but we can take advantage of them too.

As developers, we are constantly working with data—adding it, changing it, retrieving it, deleting it. The software we write is generally centered around data. The retrieving part is easy when we know specifics about the data we’re looking for. If we have an ID, a category, and a price range, traditional databases work well. Vector databases use embedding models to give us powerful search capabilities. Instead of trying to match words, we can find what we’re looking for based on the meaning of what is being asked.

### **Powering a Better Search Experience**

Advances in search technology have always centered around helping machines understand the meaning of both what’s being asked and the possible responses. LLM-powered tools like ChatGPT have made us accustomed to asking machines for information in a more natural way. We can simply ask a question, and we _usually_ get a useful answer. This hasn’t always been the case.

Early search engines relied on keywords, also known as _lexical search_, to find the information being searched for. Companies paid search engines large sums of money (and still do) for their company to be associated with certain keywords. The best developers were skilled at knowing how to string together keywords to get Google to respond with a useful coding solution. Senior developers even created acronyms to taunt juniors who struggled to find solutions through a Google search:

- **LMGTFY** Let me google that for you
- **GIYF** Google is your friend
- **JFGI** Just **** google it

It usually wasn’t that junior developers didn’t search for a solution; they just had no idea how to google effectively. It was a honed skill. Thanks to advances in search and code generation, finding a coding solution is often the easy part. The hard part is having the experience and knowledge necessary to determine whether the solution is actually a good fit and can be customized properly for your use case.

We can illustrate the drawbacks of a keyword search with a simple relational database example. Most relational databases rely on some form of Structured Query Language (SQL) to retrieve data stored in tables. While SQL is powerful and efficient, it’s not well suited to natural language queries.

Let’s take the following table containing product descriptions:

-- Create the table with an integer primary key and a text description
CREATE TABLE product_descriptions (
    id SERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
    description TEXT NOT NULL
);

We want to find all “winter jackets.” This really means we really want all heavy jackets suitable for winter regardless of whether the exact words _winter_ or _jacket_ appear. As humans, this seems obvious, but traditional search methods are much more literal.

Using SQL, you might try a LIKE statement or full-text search (depending on the SQL dialect):

-- Traditional SQL approach
SELECT *
FROM product_descriptions
WHERE description LIKE '%winter jacket%';

Or:

-- Traditional full-text search approach (MySQL/MariaDB/others)
SELECT *
FROM product_descriptions
WHERE MATCH(description) AGAINST('winter jacket' IN NATURAL LANGUAGE MODE);

The first query relies on literal words. The second option is better as it relies on words and synonyms. Unfortunately, anything talking about a “heavy coat for cold weather” or “outerwear for frigid climates” might be missed just because the text doesn’t match “winter jacket” or close synonyms.

Vector databases are different. They use special AI models, known as _embedding models_, to capture the meaning and context of natural language, images, and audio. These models create a numeric representation that machines can understand, known as a _vector embedding_. Therefore, if we query a vector database containing product descriptions with the same text, “winter jacket,” we’ll likely get the results we’re looking for. We say _likely_ because this depends on the embedding model chosen and how the data was prepared prior to creating the vector embeddings.

That’s the purpose of this chapter. We’ll start by showing conceptually how vector embeddings work and how they power natural language search. We’ll then look at examples of common use cases for vector databases beyond simply powering search or a RAG solution. By the end of this chapter, you will have a strong understanding of how to use vector databases in practice.

The examples in this chapter use the Chroma database. It’s a free, open source database that makes it easy to provide vector search capabilities for your data, but there are plenty of options to choose from. As always, you can follow along with the examples in this chapter in the GitHub repository. You can find them in the _part3/vector_databases_ folder.

### **DATABASES WITH VECTOR EMBEDDING SUPPORT**

Some relational and document databases (such as PostgreSQL, MongoDB, and Apache Cassandra) have added support for vector embeddings either directly or through extensions. This allows you to perform vector-based similarity searches directly against your data.

For example, by adding the popular pgvector extension to PostgreSQL, you can store vector embeddings along with the rest of your data as a vector type. The extension also provides SQL extensions to perform nearest neighbor and distance-related searches. If you need the similarity search capabilities described in this chapter, check the capabilities of your existing database solution before looking for a vector database.

### **Understanding Vector Embeddings**

Returning to our AI support agent, remember that we need to understand what the user is asking and retrieve any related support knowledge base articles. We mentioned that a vector database can provide these _semantic_ search results. They’re powered by embeddings. Embeddings are really the secret sauce behind the magic of LLMs. They allow machines to relate text, images, and audio.

Embedding models are specialized AI models (neural networks) trained to transform data that we as humans understand (such as text, images, and audio) into a compressed, machine-friendly encoding called a _vector embedding_. The goal is to generate a common data representation that allows a machine to understand similarities between data in the same way we do.

When a user asks, “I’m having password issues. Can you help me log in?,” we can convert that string to an embedding via an embedding model ([Figure 7-2](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#fig7_2)).

![A string reading “I’m having password issues, can you help me log in?” passes into a box that outputs the same text with a numeric array below it.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page165_1.jpg)

_Figure 7-2: An embedding model generates an embedding when given a string._

To us, the embedding looks like a fixed-length array of floating-point numbers (for example, [0.01, 0.04, 0.07, . . . , 0.05]). To an AI model, this is a mathematical vector, a point in a highly multidimensional space.

As humans, we can’t comprehend a multidimensional space larger than three dimensions. So let’s try to understand how an AI model sees these vectors from our perspective ([Figure 7-3](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#fig7_3)).

![Login and password article labels cluster near the top of a three-dimensional graph, while a subscription article label appears far below.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page166_1.jpg)

_Figure 7-3: A simplified three-dimensional view of a vector embedding space for knowledge base articles_

This graph represents the vector embeddings for our support knowledge base articles and a question the user has asked. Notice that if we see each embedding as a point in the graph, all the related login and password articles are grouped together. In contrast, the article about how to change your subscription level is graphed with a point far away from our login and password articles.

When we generate an embedding for the user question “I’m having password issues. Can you help log in?,” the point for this question would be close to our login and password articles in the graph. In this way, we know that these articles are related to what the user is asking.

Vector embeddings can represent text from multiple languages, images, audio, and more. Embedding models that handle a variety of data types as input are referred to as _multimodal_ embedding models ([Figure 7-4](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#fig7_4)). For the purposes of this book, we will remain focused on text embedding models, but the concepts are the same with a multimodal model.

![Documents, Images, and Audio labels point into an Embedding Model box, which outputs three numeric arrays labeled Vector Representation.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page166_2.jpg)

_Figure 7-4: A multimodal embedding model converting text, images, and audio to vectors_

Each floating-point number in a vector embedding represents a dimension in a highly multidimensional space. Each dimension represents a _feature_ of the data it’s trained to encode. The value of the floating-point number indicates how closely the encoded data aligns with that feature. The encoded result is a vector, a coordinate in this highly multidimensional space. We can determine how similar two of our data inputs are by measuring the distance between two vector coordinates.

Let’s look at another example to make sense of this. An apple, banana, and lemon are all fruits. But Apple is also a company name. As humans, we immediately understand these relationships, but that understanding is based on context. By itself, the word _apple_ could mean the company or the fruit. But, an article discussing the latest Apple MacBook Pro has nothing to do with fruit. Embedding models encode this context in a way a computer can understand.

[Figure 7-5](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#fig7_5) shows a three-dimensional vector space specifically designed to describe fruit. Each dimension represents a feature we can understand:

- **Sweetness (x-axis)** How sweet the fruit tastes
- **Size (y-axis)** How large the fruit is
- **Citrus (z-axis)** How “citrusy” the fruit is

We can assign vectors to fruits based on these features and plot the coordinates on a three-dimensional graph ([Figure 7-5](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#fig7_5)).

![Three-dimensional graph with Sweetness, Size, and Citrus axes plotting Banana, Apple, and Lemon with their coordinates.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page167_1.jpg)

_Figure 7-5: Mapping fruits in a three-dimensional vector representation_

[Table 7-1](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#tab7-1) shows how these vector mappings were determined.

**Table 7-1:** The Coordinates of Fruits Based on Their Descriptions
|**Fruit**|**Coordinates**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|
|Banana|(8, 7, 0)|Very sweet (8)<br><br>Moderate-to-large size (7)<br><br>Non-citrus (0)|
|Apple|(5, 6, 1)|Moderately sweet (5)<br><br>Medium size (6)<br><br>Slightly citrusy (1)|
|Lemon|(2, 4, 8)|Not very sweet (2)<br><br>Smaller size (4)<br><br>Strongly citrusy (8)|

Notice that banana and apple are closer together because they are similar in size, sweetness, and being less citrusy. Lemon is not very sweet, smaller, and strongly citrusy, so it’s farther away. Yet, the features we’ve identified are useful only for mapping fruits. It doesn’t even make sense to plot Apple, the company, on this graph. Fortunately, vector embeddings are much more sophisticated.

In reality, the numbers in a vector embedding aren’t meaningful to us. Each dimension captures a feature that the embedding model learned during training. We really don’t know what concepts the features represent. We simply know that if we measure the distance between two vector points, we can determine how contextually similar the original data is.

In general, the more dimensions, the more accurate the model. [Table 7-2](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#tab7-2) shows some OpenAI embedding models with the dimensions they encode.

Notice that we’ve included a multimodal model: clip-ViT-B-32. While we will not be showing examples of multimodel models, they can illustrate how embeddings help machines understand our world. Going back to our example, if you created an embedding of the text “apple” and an image of an apple, both would be plotted near each other. They are simply different representations of the same real-world thing.

We’ll be using Chroma (_[https://www.trychroma.com](https://www.trychroma.com/)_) as our vector database. Chroma uses Sentence-BERT (SBERT) by default for generating vector embeddings. In [Chapter 1](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch1.xhtml#ch1), we discussed BERT and its revolutionary impact on language models. SBERT is a version of BERT that turns sentences into vector embeddings.

SBERT is implemented and distributed through a Python library called Sentence Transformers (_[https://sbert.net](https://sbert.net/)_) that’s maintained by the AI community Hugging Face (_[https://huggingface.co](https://huggingface.co/)_). You can install the Python library by running the following command in your terminal window:

~ % pip install sentence-transformers==5.1.2

Our examples in this chapter are centered around a product database. Here’s how we could use the Sentence Transformers library to generate and print the vector embeddings for a list of product titles:

   from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer

➊ model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")

➋ sentences = [
     "Sony Wireless Noise-Canceling Headphones",
     "Canon Professional DSLR Camera Kit",
     "Jenson Wireless Earbuds Pro",
     "Panasonic Wireless Noise-Reduction Headphones",
   ]

➌ embeddings = model.encode(sentences)

   print(embeddings[0])
   print(f"Dimensions: {len(embeddings[0])}")

We start by initializing SentenceTransformer with an embedding model ➊. Hugging Face has many embedding models to choose from, but in this case we use all-MiniLM-L6-v2. It’s one of the most commonly used SBERT models because it strikes a strong balance between speed, size, and semantic performance. We create an array of the strings we want to encode ➋. We then pass those strings to our model to encode and print the result ➌. Pretty simple!

Running the preceding code snippet prints the first vector embedding (an array of floating-point numbers) and the number of dimensions (the length of the array):

 -5.39981276e-02  6.65992051e-02 -5.22223897e-02 -2.62297336e-02
 ... ...]
Dimensions: 384

With the number of pretrained models available, it’s simple to generate embeddings from text, images, audio, and more. We can also use Sentence Transformer to calculate the distance between vector embeddings and provide a similarity score:

   --snip--

   idx_to_compare = 0

➊ similarities = model.similarity(embeddings[idx_to_compare], embeddings)

   print(sentences[idx_to_compare])

➋ for idx, embedding in enumerate(embeddings):
  ➌ print(f"- {sentences[idx]}, Score: {similarities[idx_to_compare][idx]:.4f}")

Here we are using the model’s similarity function to calculate similarity scores for all embeddings when compared to “Sony Wireless Noise-Canceling Headphones” ➊. We use the Python enumerate function to loop through the embeddings ➋. This function allows you to loop over both the index (idx) and the item (embedding) from the embeddings array at the same time.

We then print the similarity scores with another Python feature (:.4f) to format the similarity scores to four decimal places ➌. The similarity scores are floating-point values from 0 to 1. The higher the value, the more similar the strings. Running this code snippet generates the following output:

Sony Wireless Noise-Canceling Headphones
- Sony Wireless Noise-Canceling Headphones, Score: 1.0000
- Canon Professional DSLR Camera Kit, Score: 0.0892
- Jenson Wireless Earbuds Pro, Score: 0.4783
- Panasonic Wireless Noise-Reduction Headphones, Score: 0.7500

We started by printing the string we are comparing, “Sony Wireless Noise-Canceling Headphones.” We then looped through all the embeddings and printed their similarity score. Notice that an identical match results in the highest similarity score (1.000), while an unrelated product like “Canon Professional DSLR Camera Kit” results in a low similarity score (0.0892). The “Panasonic Wireless Noise-Reduction Headphones” resulted in a high similarity score (0.75), telling us these products are related—different brands but similar products.

We will not be calculating the distance between vector embeddings to determine similarity scores on our own. We will let a vector database do that work for us, but it helps us understand what’s happening under the hood if we’re not getting the results we expect.

The choice of embedding model will have a significant impact on the results you can expect. Embedding models are trained and tuned for a particular purpose. They are also heavily influenced (biased) by their training data. The features they focus on can be entirely different depending on the model you select.

|**Key questions to ask**|**Considerations**|

### **Dissecting Model Names**

Model names are like identity cards for AI. They quickly tell you who created the model, what family it belongs to, how big it is, and what kind of training or optimization it has undergone. Understanding these segments, often separated by hyphens, helps you choose the right model for your specific needs.

When you’re looking at pretrained models on platforms like Hugging Face, their names often contain a wealth of information, acting like a compressed summary of their key characteristics. Let’s break down some examples to see what each segment means:

#### **google/gemma-3-27b-pt**

- **google/gemma** Indicates the model family is Gemma, developed by Google.
- **3** Denotes a version number or major iteration of the model architecture.
- **27b** 27 billion parameters. Parameters are the numerical values within the model that get adjusted during training; more parameters generally mean a larger, more complex model with greater learning capacity but also higher computational requirements.
- **pt** Pretrained. This signifies a base model that has been trained on a massive dataset for general language understanding and generation, but without any specific fine-tuning for particular tasks or conversational styles. It’s the raw foundation.

#### **google/gemma-3-27b-it**

- **it** Instruction-tuned. Unlike the pt (pretrained) version, this model has undergone an additional stage of training specifically to make it better at following instructions and engaging in conversational dialogue. It’s optimized for chatbot-like interactions. This is generally what you’d use for a chatbot or a model you give direct commands to.

#### **google/gemma-3-4b-it-qat-q4_0-gguf**

- **4b** 4 billion parameters. This model is significantly smaller than the 27b version, meaning it requires less memory and can run faster, often making it suitable for deployment on more accessible hardware.
- **it** Instruction-tuned, meaning it’s good at following commands and chatting, just like its larger sibling.
- **qat** Quantization-aware training. This indicates that the model was specifically trained with quantization in mind and that the model learned to be robust to the precision reduction that happens during quantization, aiming to minimize any performance loss when it’s eventually quantized.
- **q4_0** 4-bit quantization, specifically using a certain quantization scheme. Quantization is a technique that reduces the model’s size and speeds up inference by representing its numerical parameters with fewer bits (such as 4 bits instead of 16 or 32 bits). This model version is already quantized.
- **gguf** GPT-Generated Unified Format (GGUF). This is a specific file format optimized for efficient CPU (and sometimes GPU) inference, often used for running LLMs on local machines.

#### **distilbert/distilbert-base-uncased-finetuned-sst-2-english**

- **distilbert/distilbert** Indicates that the model family is DistilBERT, a smaller, faster, and lighter version of BERT.
- **base** Denotes the base configuration of the model (often a standard size, not “tiny” or “large”).
- **uncased** The text that the model was trained on ignored the difference between uppercase and lowercase letters (for example, _Apple_ and _apple_ are treated the same).
- **finetuned** A key indicator that the model has undergone fine-tuning (as discussed in previous chapters) for a specific downstream task.
- **sst-2** Specifies the particular dataset the model was fine-tuned on; in this case, the Stanford Sentiment Treebank v2 dataset, which is commonly used for sentiment analysis. This tells you the model is likely good at determining whether a piece of text expresses positive or negative sentiment.
- **english** The language the model was trained and fine-tuned on.

#### **deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B**

- **deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1** Identifies the model family as DeepSeek-R1, developed by DeepSeek AI.
- **Distill** Indicates that this model has undergone knowledge distillation, a technique that trains a smaller model (the “student”) to mimic the behavior of a much larger, more powerful model (the “teacher”). The goal is to get similar performance from a smaller, more efficient model.
- **Llama-70B** This segment often specifies the “teacher” model from which the current model was distilled, or at least indicates its lineage. In this case, the 70-billion-parameter Llama model was likely the larger model whose knowledge was transferred, implying this DeepSeek-R1 model has inherited some of its capabilities.

Getting a handle on these naming tricks means you can quickly “read” a model at a glance. You’ll know what it’s good for, whether your computer can even run it, and if it’s the right fit for whatever cool AI project you’re building.

### **How Vector Database Retrieval Works**

Vector databases are purpose built for storing vector embeddings and efficiently calculating the distance between them. We can load a vector database with millions, even billions, of embeddings representing our data. We can then query a vector database with a question or statement and retrieve the most similar data in real time.

Let’s again think back to the AI support agent example. To load a vector database with our support knowledge base articles, we would need to generate an embedding for every article ([Figure 7-6](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#fig7_6)).

![An article's text generates a numeric array, then appears inside a Vector Database alongside two other article text labels.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page174_1.jpg)

_Figure 7-6: An embedding is generated for every article in the vector database._

Notice that the related login and password articles are grouped near one another in our vector database. Once all the articles are loaded into the vector database, it can be queried to retrieve articles that are related to what a user is asking. It uses specialized indexes for this that we will discuss shortly ([Figure 7-7](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#fig7_7)).

This is why vector databases are so useful for RAG solutions. We’ll return to this in [Chapter 8](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch8.xhtml#ch8) when we build a complete RAG example.

Vector databases use a _nearest neighbor (NN) algorithm_ to determine similarity by calculating the distance between vector embeddings. When we query a vector database, it will return the _k-nearest neighbors (k-NN)_, where _k_ is the number of closest results we are expecting. [Figure 7-8](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#fig7_8) illustrates a similarity search using vector embeddings.

![Documents, Images, and Audio become vector arrays, undergo a Nearest Neighbor comparison against a query vector, and return results.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page175_2.jpg)

_Figure 7-8: A vector database similarity search adapted from_ [https://www.elastic.co/what-is/vector-embedding](https://www.elastic.co/what-is/vector-embedding)

Vector databases generally use an _approximate nearest neighbor (ANN)_ search algorithm to calculate these distances efficiently. Several algorithms can do this, but they really just use clever shortcuts like indexing and clustering to find results fast.

The shortcuts of the ANN search algorithm mean the results of a search will be very close but aren’t guaranteed to be exact. Just keep this tradeoff of efficiency over absolute correctness in mind when choosing a vector database for your solution.

### **ENN VS. ANN: EFFICIENCY OVER ABSOLUTE CORRECTNESS**

_Exact nearest neighbor (ENN)_ vector searches calculate all distances across all indexed vectors so the closest neighbors will be retrieved based on a distance metric. This means your results are guaranteed to be accurate.

While ENN is supported by some vector databases, it’s highly inefficient for large numbers of vector embeddings and should be considered only when the number of embeddings is low and precision is of high importance. ANN search algorithms are efficient enough to use in real time with a high-dimensional vector space and millions or even billions of vector embeddings.

### **Using a Vector Database for Meaningful Search Results**

Let’s expand our previous example by loading a vector database with our list of products. Our script will need to do the following:

- Retrieve all products from a data source
- Initialize an embedding provider (we’ll start with our all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model)
- Create a vector database client that uses our embedding provider
- Create descriptive text for each product that will be used to generate an embedding
- Add all the products to the vector database along with product attributes as metadata so they’re indexed

#### _**Creating the Product Collection**_

In Chroma, a _collection_ is the main container you work with. It’s a named grouping of documents (or other records), their embeddings (vectors), and any metadata stored alongside them. Before we can create a collection, we’ll need a few more Python libraries:

- **chromadb** Provides an interface to Chroma
- **pandas** Library designed for data analysis and manipulation
- **NumPy** Core library for numerical computing in Python

Open a terminal and run the following command:

~/ai-for-devs % pip install chromadb==1.2.1 pandas==2.3.3 numpy==1.26.4

Once the libraries are installed, we can initialize our Chroma client and a product_search collection for our products:

import chromadb
from chromadb.utils import embedding_functions

client = chromadb.Client()

embedding_function = embedding_functions.SentenceTransformerEmbeddingFunction(
  model_name="all-MiniLM-L6-v2"
)

collection = client.create_collection(
  name="product_search",
  embedding_function=embedding_function
)

Notice that we configure the collection with embedding_function. The collection will handle the embedding generation for us; we simply have to tell it the embedding model to use. Here, we’re using a simple Chroma wrapper around SentenceTransformer configured to use the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model we saw previously.

Chroma provides wrappers around popular embedding providers that can be found in its embedding_functions package. Can’t find the embedding provider you’re looking for? You can create your own by implementing the EmbeddingFunction protocol. This is a common pattern with vector databases, allowing you to choose the embedding model that best fits your needs.

### **CRUD OPERATIONS ON A VECTOR DATABASE**

Just like the databases you’re used to, vector databases allow for create, read, update, and delete (CRUD) operations. Chroma provides collection APIs for the following:

### **Using a Vector Database to Make Recommendations**

Now that we have a vector database that contains our products, we can expand its use. Recall that each embedding captures the semantic understanding of a product. Therefore, if we have a product title and description, we could use our vector database to find other related products.

For an e-commerce site, this has some pretty helpful use cases:

- **Recommending related products** When showing a product, we may want to show other related products so the user can make comparisons or be enticed to make an additional purchase.
- **Recommending products based on purchase history** If we know a user’s purchase history, we can use the products they have purchased to find similar products the user may be interested in.
- **Classifying new products** It’s common to filter products by category (such as apparel, electronics, or home furnishings). When a user is adding a new product, we can find similar products to help us recommend a proper category.

In all cases, our vector database search would look something like [Figure 7-9](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch7.xhtml#fig7_9).

![A product title and description become a numeric array and query the Vector Database for two results, each with metadata like product ID and category.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page181_1.jpg)

_Figure 7-9: A vector database query for products similar to a product title and description_

We take a product or list of products and combine their titles and descriptions into a string representation. The vector database converts our string into an embedding in order to find the two (top_k=2) most similar product embeddings. Remember that each embedding includes the product metadata necessary to identify the product (product_id) and its assigned category. We will come back to this later.

We are adding to a database and querying. This is something we all understand as developers. Of course, we could train a traditional ML model to do this, but a vector database solution has several advantages:

- **Simplicity** Instead of training an ML model, we use a pretrained embedding model.
- **Interpretability** We can see exactly which existing products are most similar to past purchases.
- **Flexibility** The system can easily handle new products and categories without retraining.
- **Relevance scoring** The relevance scores are based on similarity and product category.

Let’s look at an example. We will use our vector database to analyze a user’s purchase history and make product recommendations. As always, you can follow along with the example in the GitHub repository folder _part3/ vector_databases_.

Our vector database is already loaded with the products, but we need some new classes. Let’s define those first.

#### _**Identifying the Most Relevant Results**_

Now we will process the results to determine relevance scores for the products returned:

   --snip--

     recommendations = []
    
  ➊ for idx, metadata in enumerate(results['metadatas'][0]):
    ➋ product_id = metadata['product_id']
        
       # Skip if this product was already purchased
    ➌ if purchase_history.has_purchased_product(product_id):
         continue

    ➏ product.relevance_score = results['distances'][0][idx] + category_bonus

         break

     # Sort by relevance score (lower is better)
  ➐ recommendations.sort(key=lambda x: x.relevance_score)

We start by looping through the metadata for all the products returned by our query ➊. Within our loop, we get the product_id from the product metadata so we can identify the product being recommended ➋. We then use that product_id to exclude any product the user has already purchased ➌.

Now we give the bonus weight to any products that are in the same category as products the user has previously purchased ➍. This is a strategy to narrow down to products the user might be interested in. We use the find_product method with the product_id to retrieve the recommended product ➎. We then set a relevance_score on the product and append it to a list of recommendations. The relevance_score is a combination of the distance that Chroma calculated between the product embeddings it found and the products we provided plus our category bonus ➏.

Finally, we sort our recommendations with the most related at the top and return the results ➐. This is a form of _re-ranking_ our results. We will discuss this more in [Chapter 8](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch8.xhtml#ch8) when we cover more advanced RAG topics.

### **VECTOR DATABASE DISTANCE CALCULATIONS**

We keep talking about this vector distance calculation, but what do we mean? The default distance calculation for Chroma is L2 (Euclidean) distance. This is like the straight-line distance between two points in our highly dimensional space.

For the other two supported calculations, think of drawing a line from the graph origin to a vector embedding point. The cosine distance calculation prioritizes the direction of this line. The ip (inner product) calculation would prioritize both the direction and length of vectors.

You can experiment with different distance calculations by setting the hnsw:space metadata option when initializing your collection:

collection = client.create_collection(
  name="products",
  embedding_function=embedding_function,
  metadata={"hnsw:space": "cosine"}
)

This option impacts how the collection is created, so just like your embedding _function, it can be set only at the time you create your collection. For more configuration settings, check the documentation for your vector database solution.

#### _**Filtering on Metadata**_

So far, our recommendations have focused entirely on the similarity between item embeddings—how close two products are in vector space. But that’s only part of the story. Real-world recommendations often need to consider additional context about the user.

If we’ve captured user information such as age range and location (demographics) or shopping preferences, we can make our recommendations much more relevant. For example, we probably don’t want to recommend hiking boots to someone who lives in a major downtown area and never buys outdoor gear.

This is where metadata filtering comes in. In Chroma, we can apply a where filter to our query, allowing us to limit search results based on metadata values. Metadata filters give us an additional layer of control, letting us combine semantic similarity with contextual relevance.

> ### **NOTE**
> 
> _You can also filter by document contents with the_ where_document _filter._

We could modify our example to add a filter that restricts results to only the product categories a user has purchased from before:

   --snip--

➊ purchase_categories = [
     product.category for product in purchase_history.products ]

   # Get recommendations based on description similarity and category metadata
   results = self.collection.query(
     query_texts=purchase_history.get_purchase_descriptions(),
     n_results=n_recommendations,
  ➋ where={"category": {"$in": purchase_categories}}
   )

   --snip--

Metadata filters work a lot like SQL queries. They let you narrow results based on metadata conditions rather than just vector similarity. You can combine multiple criteria, compare values, and even filter by ranges or lists.

Here, we gather the categories for products the user has purchased in the past ➊. We then add a where filter that filters the results to only products with one of those category values in their metadata ➋.

A where filter supports common logical and comparison operations such as these:

- **Logical operators** (**$and, $or)** Combine multiple conditions (for example, products in the electronics category and under $100).
- **Comparison operators** (**$eq, $gt, $lt)** Compare numeric or text values directly.
- **Set operators** (**$in, $nin)** Match one of several possible values (for example, categories in [books, games]).

Together, these operators let you build powerful queries that combine vector similarity with structured filtering. In this way, you can recommend items that are not only similar but also relevant to the user’s profile or past behavior.

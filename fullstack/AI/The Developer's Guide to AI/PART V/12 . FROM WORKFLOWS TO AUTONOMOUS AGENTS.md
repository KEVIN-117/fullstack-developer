We’ve explored model architectures, dissected LLMs, experimented with RAG, crafted prompts, and fine-tuned performance, yet our interactions have been purely reactive, confined to chat windows or one-off scripts. That changes now.

The true potential of AI extends far beyond these limitations. Imagine empowering AI to act independently, to execute tasks on your behalf. Say, for instance, it could compose and send an email, process the recipient’s reply, summarize its content, and take an action on it—all without direct human intervention.

This vision demands a paradigm shift, moving from passive interactions to active autonomy. We need to give these LLMs a level of agency. We need to liberate LLMs from the chat window and our custom programs, granting them the ability to interact with the real world. This requires equipping them with the tools to access search engines, manipulate programs, and ultimately, solve complex tasks with agency. Agentic AI, with its sophisticated agent technologies, serves as the gateway, bridging the gap between the computational power of LLMs and the dynamic reality of our world.

### **What Are AI Agents?**

An AI agent is a system that uses an LLM as its “brain” to autonomously plan, reason, and execute a series of steps to achieve a specific goal. Unlike a traditional chat-based model that simply responds to a prompt, an agent is given a high-level task and then independently figures out how to accomplish it.

Think of the difference this way: A _chat-based model_ is like an expert you can ask questions. You ask, “What are the best vegan restaurants in my area?,” and it gives you a list. It’s a single, reactive turn of conversation. An _AI agent_ is like an autonomous assistant. You give it the goal: “Find a highly rated vegan restaurant with a patio that is open this Saturday, and make a reservation for two.” The agent will then break this into a series of steps:

- **Planning** It will determine the steps needed to achieve the goal. It might decide to first search for vegan restaurants, then filter them by rating and patio availability, and finally use a tool to check for reservations.
- **Tool use** It will use a search engine tool to find restaurants, a calendar tool to check the date, and a reservation tool to book the table.
- **Reasoning** It will analyze the results from each step. If a restaurant is closed, the agent will reason that it needs to go back and find another one. If it successfully finds a place and books the reservation, it will conclude the task.

In essence, an AI agent transforms a passive LLM into an active, goal-oriented problem solver by giving it access to a suite of tools and a framework to use them effectively.

### **Why Do We Need Agents?**

Before we try to answer the question, we must understand the difference between a traditional workflow, an AI workflow, and an agentic workflow, which is a workflow that uses one or more agents.

A _workflow_ is a sequence of interconnected steps or tasks designed to achieve a specific business outcome or goal. It outlines who does what, when they do it, and what information or resources are involved in each stage. Workflows provide structure, consistency, and often automation to processes, helping to streamline operations, improve efficiency, and ensure a repeatable and predictable way of completing tasks. They can range from simple, linear processes to complex, branching sequences involving multiple individuals, systems, and decisions.

With AI, we now have (or could define) three kinds of workflows, depending on the level of autonomy that we have given the AI or LLM:

- **Traditional workflows** No AI
- **AI workflows** AI used in a predetermined order
- **Agentic workflows** A goal oriented workflow, with nondeterminism in its sequence of steps

#### _**Traditional Workflows**_

A traditional workflow is a predefined, rule-based sequence of automated tasks or data transformations, typically implemented using tools such as extract, transform, load (ETL) processes, business process management (BPM) systems, or integration platforms (integration platform as a service, or iPaaS). These workflows are deterministic, meaning they follow fixed logic, and are designed to automate repetitive tasks or data movement between applications.

They rely on explicit, preconfigured rules and logic, lacking the adaptive or decision-making capabilities of AI-driven workflows. Traditional workflows are often triggered by specific events or schedules and are designed to ensure data consistency and process efficiency across systems ([Figure 12-1](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch12.xhtml#fig12_1)).

_Figure 12-1: Traditional workflow_

Traditional workflows are defined by their rigid, predefined steps, often relying on rule-based systems that excel at predictable, repetitive tasks. However, these systems frequently struggle with adaptability and complex decision-making, often requiring human intervention to manage exceptions or navigate unforeseen circumstances.

An example is a simple script that automatically sorts and files documents based on predefined criteria.

#### _**AI Workflow**_

An AI workflow is a structured, linear sequence of tasks that applies AI tools in a predetermined order to achieve a specific outcome. Each step in the workflow is clearly defined, and the output of one step serves as the input for the next. This approach emphasizes predictability and control, making it ideal for processes that require consistency and reliability. AI workflows are deterministic, meaning that given the same input, they will always produce the same output ([Figure 12-2](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch12.xhtml#fig12_2)).

![User input flows through steps to an LLM that generates a response, then continues to Done.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page279_2.jpg)

_Figure 12-2: AI workflow_

AI-driven workflows transform automation by incorporating intelligent models that go beyond simple logic to enhance complex decision-making. These systems leverage machine learning to adapt to changing conditions and process nuanced tasks that traditional rule-based systems can’t handle. However, while they provide significant efficiency, they are not fully autonomous and still rely on human oversight for critical decisions and strategic guidance.

An example is a system that uses AI to route customer support tickets based on sentiment analysis but that still requires human agents to resolve complex issues.

#### _**Agentic Workflow**_

An agentic workflow incorporates AI agents that can make autonomous decisions and take actions to achieve a goal within the workflow. While this workflow still has an overall structure, the agents have flexibility in choosing which tools to use and how to proceed at certain stages. This introduces a degree of nondeterminism, as the agent’s choices can vary based on the situation.

Agentic workflows are suitable for more complex tasks that might require adaptability and problem-solving, but they also demand careful monitoring and control to ensure that the agent stays aligned with the desired outcome ([Figure 12-3](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/the-developers-guide/0642572230333/xhtml/ch12.xhtml#fig12_3)).

![User input flows to an AI Agent that cycles through Think, Plan, Execute with Tools, and Reflect before reaching Done.](https://learning.oreilly.com/api/v2/epubs/urn:orm:book:0642572230333/files/images/annot_page280_1.jpg)

_Figure 12-3: Agentic workflow_

Agentic workflows represent a leap beyond simple automation by empowering LLMs with genuine autonomy and agency. These systems can independently plan, execute, and adapt to complex tasks, leveraging external tools and environmental feedback to achieve specific goals without constant human intervention. By interacting with their surroundings and accessing real-time information, agentic workflows can even learn and refine their processes over time, becoming increasingly efficient as they navigate unpredictable challenges.

An example is an AI agent that manages your travel itinerary, booking flights, hotels, and activities based on your preferences, while proactively adapting to changes and providing real-time updates.

### **Summary**

We shifted our perspective from reactive AI interactions to the proactive capabilities of AI agents. You learned that while LLMs, RAG, prompting, and fine-tuning are powerful for generating responses, true AI potential lies in getting models to act independently, orchestrate tasks, and interact with the real world. This necessitates moving beyond simple chat interfaces to giving LLMs a level of agency.

We then explored the fundamental differences between three types of workflows: traditional workflows (rigid, rule-based), AI workflows (incorporating AI in predetermined steps), and agentic workflows (where AI agents make autonomous decisions and take actions to achieve goals, introducing nondeterminism and adaptability). This distinction highlighted why AI agents are crucial for tackling complex, multi-step tasks that require dynamic problem-solving and tool utilization, paving the way for AI to become an active participant in intricate digital processes.
